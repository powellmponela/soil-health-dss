This branching contrasts with Wezel et al., (2020) , who aggregated the biophysical principles into two pathways (resource efficiency vs resilience). The dendrogram indicates finer, non-binary structure in which several principles form stable micro-constellations rather than collapsing neatly into the two macro-pathways: recycling and biodiversity share lower branching consistent with resilience pathway, while animal health, input reduction and synergy branch at incrementally higher node heights – constructed more like cross-cutting enablers standalone principles. On the socio-economic cluster : first order constructs aggregate into three pathways: (i) co-creation of knowledge and participation, (ii) fairness, connectivity, and land and natural resource governance, and (iii) economic diversification, and social values and diets. Pathways (ii) and (iii) merging at higher nodes may indicate a common governance-market orientation. The study posit s that biophysical convergence reflects the historical evolution of evaluation paradigms. Following a prolonged period of productivism, yield-centric evaluation, “ second wave ” frameworks standardized field-level soil and agronomic metrics - soil organic matter, erosion control, nutrient use - because they were relatively tractable to measure and had clear plot-scale causal links (Andrews et al. 2004) . Socio-economic dimensions were integrated later as attention shifted from plots to farms, landscapes, and food systems, where operationalization required deliberation, institutional arrangements, and context-specific value choices (HLPE 2019; Wezel et al. 2020) . Consequently, most frameworks share a common biophysical framing but diverge along a social–economic one . This asymmetry has direct implications for design, action and monitoring. Since biophysical “indicators” are broadly similar across the assessed frameworks, strategic distinctiveness - and much of the impact variance - arises from socio-economic differences. Effective designs pair the shared biophysical principles with socio-technical contexts: emphasize co-creation where legitimacy and uptake are binding; strengthen fairness, connectivity, and governance rules where coordination and inclusion are weak; and promote diversification, value addition, and diet-quality incentives where demand and profitability are low (Dumont et al. 2021) . Understanding the asymmetry, the stakeholders can be guided to: (i) measure what they design and practice: that is, bundling indicators so each practice set is paired with enabling-environment metrics for scaling; (ii) keep plot-scale agronomic indicators to capture short-run change, and (iii) add governance at farm, landscape, and food-system scales to track sustainability, diffusion, and equity. D esign domains The row clustering of REF _Ref199757103 \h \* MERGEFORMAT Figure 3 delineates five framework design domains – (i) soil management and input stewardship, (ii) soil health assessment, (iii) agroecological and ecosystem-based approaches, (iv) integrated landscape and livelihoods, and (v) policy and outcome-oriented. The se five design domains translate complex clustering results into an interpretable typology of how frameworks operationalize soil health within agri-food system s’ transitions, supporting clearer selection, combination, and sequencing for planning and evaluation. Soil management and input stewardship A cluster of frameworks focuses on farm-level resource efficiency, providing operational guidance and measurement routines - soil health practice guidelines (SOM maintenance, pH or salinity correction, compaction avoidance, infiltration, cover and erosion control) are linked to threshold scoring approaches that make outcomes measurable (FAO-ITPS 2017; Moebius-Clune et al. 2017) . Public guidance and standards reinforce monitoring for sustainable soil management (Faber et al. 2022; EEA 2023) , with broader strategies framing soil protection to meet regional policy targets (European Commission 2021) . Within farms, nutrient stewardship emphasizes right source, right rate, right time and right place (4R) decisions and integrated organic inputs to raise uptake while curbing losses and protecting water quality (Musumba et al. 2017; Vanlauwe et al. 2023) , while private-sector frameworks align incentives toward restoring SOM, improving nutrient cycling, and lowering synthetic inputs, with co-benefits for climate resilience and farmer livelihoods (Nestle 2024) . Although soil management and input stewardship frameworks supply indicators to verify efficiency gains , they place less emphasis on the enabling conditions: biodiversity and recycling typically enter as due-diligence items or co-benefits of practice (rotations, cover, and organic amendments) rather than primary design features ( Neßhöver et al. 2012) . Fairness, connectivity, and animal health appear where standards or reporting instruments are included (FAO-ITPS 2017; Moebius-Clune et al. 2017) , but co-creation, participation, and diet or consumption elements are comparatively muted across this stewardship -oriented set. Emphasis remains at micro level; landscape hydrology and cross-farm coordination are rarely integral , limiting their capacity to sustain soil health outcomes beyond plot or farm scale , and require complementing tools to address adoption constraints . Soil - Health Assessment The soil - health assessment frameworks are mixed and bimodal. One pole comprises agronomic toolkits that center on soil health, input efficiency, and management synergies. These include indicator suites, scoring systems, and field protocols that translate the soil triad into operational diagnostics (IFA 2009; Stockdale et al. 2019; Lehmann et al. 2020) - biology (active and total carbon, enzymes, microbial biomass), chemistry (pH, macro- or micro - nutrients), and physics (bulk density, penetration resistance, aggregate stability, available water capacity) - with scoring curves, critical limits, or minimum data sets to flag constraints and guide remediation (Arshad and Martin 2002; Andrews et al. 2004; FAO-ITPS 2020; USDA-NRCS 2021; Govindakrishnan et al. 2021; Deel et al. 2024) . The other pole takes social-ecological and health framing, focusing on fairness or participation, connectivity, and diversification while treating biophysical metrics as contextual. Regional or context-specific frameworks and open databases enable adaptation monitoring and evaluation (Jian et al. 2020; Devine et al. 2021; Ros et al. 2022) , with variants focused on water-limited environments and soil organic carbon (Nunes et al. 2021; Ghimire et al. 2023) . Recent studies position soil health within one-health or planetary-health agendas, broadening the purpose of diagnostics beyond agronomy (Lehmann et al. 2020; Montgomery et al. 2024) . Much as a ssessment frameworks offer a diagnostic spine (protocols, thresholds, scoring, datasets) that can anchor monitoring and adaptive management . Agroecological and Ecosystem-Based Agroecological and ecosystem-based frameworks pivot from plot-level practices to landscape and system governance, emphasizing co-creation or participation, connectivity and tenure or governance, and multifunctional outcomes (FAO 2018) . It couples agroecological practice with territorial coordination, ecosystem-service accounting, and participatory performance tools, moving the locus of change from fields to food systems. One subset focuses on landscape articulation of spatial coordination through stakeholder platforms (FAO 2017b, a) , restoration and long-term value creation ( Commonland 2024) , and system-level feedback ( Tittonell 2023) . The food-systems sub-block locates farm practices within enabling policy and market settings (FAO and INRAE 2020) and within regional development agendas (NEPAD 2003) . Another subset consolidates environmental targets to anchor soil and land stewardship in accounting and planning (Cowie et al. 2018) , while ecosystem-service framings provide the benefits ledger and trade-off criterion for design and evaluation (Alcamo et al. 2003; Millennium Ecosystem Assessment 2005) . Participatory performance tools operationalize co-creation and learning - from plot to biomes - through context-specific diagnostics and indicators (FAO 2019) . Relative to management and stewardship frameworks, soil functions are secured indirectly - through diversity, ground cover, water regulation, and circular nutrient flows. Agroecological and ecosystem-service approaches promote permanent cover, intercropping, N-fixing species, integrated crop–livestock systems, and reuse of organic resources (Alcamo et al. 2003; Holmgren 2017; FAO 2019) . Landscape instruments strengthen erosion control and hydrological regulation via mosaics of land uses, riparian buffers, terraces, and restoration zones, and they institutionalize cross-farm coordination (NEPAD 2003; FAO 2017a; Cowie et al. 2018; Commonland 2024) . Within this domain, farm-level nutrient guidance is retained but is explicitly embedded in system design - as one lever among diversification, agroecological redesign, and social organization (Sanginga and Woomer 2009; Tittonell 2023) . Biodiversity, recycling or circularity, and land and natural-resource governance dominate, while farm-scale input-reduction and within-farm “synergy” register weakly. Fairness, connectivity, tenure, and coordination are expressed through collective rules and planning (NEPAD 2003; Cowie et al. 2018; FAO and INRAE 2020) , co-creation or participation appears where participatory performance tools structure co-learning across scales (FAO 2019) , while diet or consumption elements are secondary and often contingent on food system policies rather than field protocols (FAO and INRAE 2020) . Agroecological and ecosystem-service frameworks embed soil health within territorial governance, coordination, and multifunctionality, shifting the locus of change from field practices to systems organization. Their strength is institutionalizing collective action and landscape integrity, while detailed agronomic routines are typically delegated to complementary toolkits. Integrated Landscape and Livelihood Emerging, this domain groups broad, integrative frameworks that address multiple dimensions (environmental, social, economic) and multiple scales (from farm to landscape to national) in a systems perspective. These frameworks fuse food-system indicator s et s with landscape or governance instruments. Agriculture and natural resource management are considered as part of a complex socio-ecological system – factoring in livelihoods, governance, equity, and long-term system change. The unifying characteristic is a holistic view that bridges traditional sectoral silos, aiming for simultaneous progress in productivity, poverty reduction, and environmental sustainability. Despite the scaling potential, it is worth noting that the geographic coverage and intended applications may vary widely. Frameworks originating in the Global North can encode institutional assumptions distinct from those co-designed in smallholder contexts. Co-creation, participation, fairness, and connectivity are built in via livelihoods , food-system s and political economy lenses (Scoones 1998; López- Ridaura et al. 2002; iPES 2015; Food System Transformative Integrated Policy (FS-TIP) initiative 2021; Posthumus et al. 2021; van der Ploeg 2021; Triomphe et al. 2022) . Land- and natural-resource governance is formalized through integrated landscape management and restoration planning (Sayer et al. 2013; Cowie et al. 2018; SKI 2021; 1000 Landscapes for 1 Billion People 2022a, b) . Biodiversity and ecosystem-service accounting anchor benefit accounting and monitoring benchmarks (Haines-Young and Potschin -Young 2018; UNEP-WCMC 2021) . Diets and economic diversification appear through food-systems analysis and agroecology reviews rather than design features ( iPES 2015; Wezel et al. 2020; Food System Transformative Integrated Policy (FS-TIP) initiative 2021) . Animal health is sparsely covered (Johnston and Bruulsema 2014; AGRF 2020; FAO-OEWG 2022; Rhees et al. 2024) . Soil outcomes are also secured indirectly - via diversified land use (e.g., agroforestry), ground cover at scale, reduced soil erosion, and improved infiltration - while technical specifics (pH or salinity correction, traffic management, nutrient timing) are typically delegated to complementary agronomic toolkits (Sayer et al. 2013; Plieninger et al. 2020) . Integrated frameworks bridge scales and dimensions to support transformation agendas, but their applicability depends on contextual fit and often requires coupling with soil-specific diagnostic and management tools to translate broad objectives into actionable soil stewardship. Policy and outcome-orien tation The high-level policy frameworks and outcome-based standards set targets, indicators, or broad principles to guide sustainable agriculture and resource management. They are often driven by governments, international bodies, or industry coalitions and serve as “reference frameworks” for aligning strategies and monitoring progress toward sustainability goals. The common thread is establishing measurable goals or commitments – whether for soil health, biodiversity, or agricultural performance – and creating accountability through indicators and reporting. Policy and convention instruments provide the anchor for tracking global obligations, continental agendas and national commitments while sectoral standards translate these into soil health target or threshold and regenerative outcome metrics (NEPAD 2011; UNEP 2011; SAI Platform 2023; Matson et al. 2024) . Soil health is framed via high-level targets, thresholds, or minimum safeguards (e.g., indicator “floors” for organic carbon, erosion risk, or basic biodiversity). The emphasis is on measurable goals, indicators, and reporting - mainstreaming biodiversity and soil health through key performance indicators - rather than on detailed, farm-level practice and measurement guidelines (Pinstrup‐Andersen and Hazell 1985) . In this configuration, soil health, input reduction, and biodiversity function mainly as baseline safeguards (e.g., indicator floors); recycling, synergy, co-creation or participation, and consumption-side elements are largely absent. The domain strengthen s accountability through targets and comparability yet risk s superficial compliance if not linked to diagnostics and practice bundles . The greatest value is the enabling monitoring–reporting–verification through which soil stewardship can be incentivized and aligned across programs. Integration and programming Content analysis and clustering indica te that framework s tend to be design ed following complementar ity, not substitution . M etrics increasingly converge around shared biophysical and agroecological principles , which creates a stronger basis for more coherent and integrated assessment systems (Dumont et al. 2016) . H istorically , framework development appears to have evolved in response to emerging needs: from an initial emphasis on soil health measurement and management, to landscape-based scaling, and more recently to outcome - , governance - and stewardship -oriented approaches . Rather than representing competing paradigms, the framework clusters can be understood as complementary design domains , each addressing distinct constraints encountered during farming - system transition . The guide is intended to support stepwise programming rather than a one-framework-per-domain selection exercise. For a given intervention, the process begins by defining the purpose of the assessment, the decision context, and the spatial scale. Policy- and outcome-oriented frameworks can be used to set measurable goals, performance criteria, and accountability mechanisms. At this stage, users should identify a limited number of critical points that either weaken or strengthen soil health, drawing on literature review, expert consultation, and participatory diagnosis. This first filtering step is important to avoid an overabundance of metrics and to focus only on the most decision-relevant domains and indicators. Once priorities are identified, stakeholders can select one or several frameworks within each relevant domain and extract candidate indicators from them. The aim is not to adopt entire frameworks wholesale, but to use them as structured sources of indicators, concepts, and measurement logic. Indicators can then be screened for relevance, feasibility, interpretability, and redundancy, producing a parsimonious set adapted to the intervention context. In this way, the process moves across domains while retaining only those elements that contribute directly to the assessment objective. Soil health assessment frameworks are particularly useful at this stage for establishing baseline diagnostics and standardized measurement protocols. The methods and tools used to assess soil health and related agroecological indicators should be adapted to local conditions and translated into forms that are meaningful to a wider audience, including farmers, extension agents, and local decision-makers. Soil stewardship and management frameworks can then be used to identify and test practice options and operational routines. Rather than prescribing a fixed intervention package, the compendium and decision matrix should be used as a basket of options from which context-appropriate combinations can be selected. Field and farm segmentation is important here, because different practices may be suitable for different farming systems, resource endowments, and landscape units. For example, in a maize-based smallholder system, programming might combine: (i) condition indicators such as soil organic matter, infiltration, and nutrient balance from a Soil health assess framework; (ii) management indicators such as residue retention, crop rotation, or organic amendments from a Soil steward framework; (iii) biodiversity and resilience indicators from an AE-ecosystem framework; and (iv) equity, market access, or governance indicators from Landscape-livelihood and Policy-outcome frameworks. These indicators would then be consolidated into one context-specific monitoring set aligned with the intervention’s goals and practical data constraints. Scaling should then be organized within agroecological and ecosystem boundaries so that adoption does not undermine biophysical integrity or intensify trade-offs as interventions expand. Local scaling partners play a central role in co-designing this strategy. Because landscape boundaries are nested, overlapping, and socially differentiated, integrated programming requires frameworks that address both ecological and social dimensions. The compendium therefore supports not only biophysical targeting, but also the integration of governance, market linkages, and livelihood objectives. Although Figure 4 presents a sequenced learning cycle, the process is not strictly linear. Spatial scales are nested or overlapping, and feedback s occur through shorter iterative loops of learning, course correction, and adaptation. Assessing the relevance, feasibility, and accuracy of selected metrics and indicators should therefore be part of the adaptive learning process itself. By reframing frameworks as complementary design domains linked through an adaptive programming cycle, research-for-development can operationalize soil health as a systems-level organizing principle rather than as a stand-alone assessment category. Conclusion Soil health is widely recognized as an important biophysical and socio-economic dimension of agroecological and agri-food system transitions, yet the proliferation of frameworks has contributed to fragmented guidance and uneven integration across assessment and design efforts. This review offers a comparative structure for examining heterogeneous framework families and for considering how institutions position soil-related indicators within broader system priorities. Across the compendium, variation among frameworks appears to reflect not only methodological differences, but also institutional strategies shaped by utility considerations and normative orientations that influence problem framing, indicator selection, and the balance between technical performance and social legitimacy. T he analysis suggests that many frameworks converge around a set of shared biophysical principles, while showing greater variation in socio-economic dimensions. This pattern may indicate that differences in governance, inclusion, learning processes, and market conditions are particularly important in shaping how soil health is interpreted and operationalized in practice. The five design domains proposed here translate these patterns into a working typology. Stewardship and assessment domains tend to support practice guidance and diagnostic routines; agroecological or ecosystem, and integrated landscape or livelihood, domains help situate scaling within territorial and governance contexts; and policy or outcome domains can help frame targets, accountability, and comparability across monitoring and reporting systems. Because these domains appear to address different but related constraints, soil stewardship may be better supported through sequenced combinations of frameworks than through reliance on a single framework alone. On this basis, instead of proposing another standalone framework, the review outlines a programming pathway that begins with policy or outcome targets, links them to soil health diagnostics, uses stewardship-oriented frameworks to inform practice options, considers scaling within agroecological or ecosystem boundaries, extends analysis to landscape and livelihood contexts, and returns to diagnostics through iterative learning. This sequencing is intended as a practical way of using complementarity across frameworks and may help position soil health more centrally within efforts to support resilient, equitable, and sustainable agri-food system transition s .
